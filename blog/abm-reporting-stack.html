Where each ABM number should live: CRM, ad platform, or in between

Most reporting arguments are really ownership arguments. Deciding which system is authoritative for which number, once, removes about half of them.

SO Sam Okonjo · 19 June 2026 · 8 min read
AI & tooling
On this pageThe three systemsWho owns which numberThe joins that go wrongA reporting layer that holds

The recurring meeting where marketing's numbers and sales' numbers disagree is rarely about analytics. It is about the fact that three systems each hold a partial view, nobody has declared which is authoritative for what, and each team is quoting the system that is most convenient to them.

Deciding ownership once, in writing, resolves most of it.

The three systems and what each is actually for

The ad platform

Authoritative for: spend, impressions, clicks, video views, creative-level delivery, audience match rate.

Not authoritative for: anything about accounts, anything about pipeline. Platform-reported conversions are useful for optimisation and should never be quoted as business outcomes — they are attributed on the platform's own model, in the platform's favour, on a window it chose.

The CRM

Authoritative for: accounts, opportunities, amounts, stages, close dates, ownership. If it is money, the CRM wins, and marketing should stop arguing about this permanently.

Not authoritative for: why an opportunity exists. Self-reported source fields and last-touch attribution are directionally interesting and precisely wrong. Nobody's CRM knows that the CFO saw four ads before the referral conversation happened.

The account layer in between

Authoritative for: the join. Which accounts were reached, which functions engaged, what each account saw and when, engagement stage and score, cost per account reached and moved.

This is the layer most stacks are missing, and its absence is why the argument keeps happening. Without it, the only available join between spend and revenue is a session-level one that cannot survive a four-month buying cycle.

The joins that go wrong

Company name to CRM account

The single most common failure. "Ravensbourne", "Ravensbourne Group" and "Ravensbourne Logistics Holdings Ltd" are three strings and one company. Join on normalised domain wherever possible, keep an alias table, and treat the unresolved list as a work queue rather than as an acceptable residual.

Person to company

Personal email addresses on form fills, consultants, contractors, and people who changed jobs three months ago. Any join that depends on email domain will lose a meaningful share of real contacts and misattribute a few.

Subsidiary to parent

Decide the rule once and apply it in every system. The failure is inconsistency: the ad platform targeting the parent, the CRM holding subsidiaries, and the account layer rolling up differently from both. That produces three defensible numbers that do not match.

A useful discipline: every reported number should carry its system of record and its definition in the same line. "Influenced pipeline £2.11m (account layer, CRM values, engaged touch within 90 days)" takes eight extra words and ends most of the arguments before they start.

A reporting layer that holds up

Four properties, in rough order of importance:

  1. Account-first. Every metric computed from the target list outward, so unreached accounts are rows rather than absences.
  2. Definitions visible in the interface. If a reader has to ask what a number means, the definition is in the wrong place. It belongs next to the number, not in a data dictionary nobody opens.
  3. Queryable by machines. An API, and increasingly an MCP endpoint, so that the reporting layer can be interrogated by your own tooling rather than exported to a spreadsheet that immediately goes stale.
  4. Historically stable. A number computed for Q1 should return the same value when someone recomputes it in Q3. Models change, and a layer that silently backfills new logic onto old periods destroys trust in every number it has ever produced.

That last one is the least glamorous and the most important. Most reporting credibility is not lost through a wrong number. It is lost when a number that was right in March is different in June and nobody can explain why.
