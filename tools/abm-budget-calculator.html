This sizes reach, not outcomes. It answers “can I afford to be seen by this list at this frequency”, which is the question most programmes get wrong before creative is ever discussed. It assumes even delivery; in practice the auction concentrates impressions unless you cap frequency and split tiers into separate campaigns.

Reading the result

Three ways the number comes back wrong

The budget is far above what you have

The correct response is almost always to cut the list, not the frequency. Reaching 150 accounts properly beats reaching 400 accounts occasionally, and the second option produces a report that looks fine and a pipeline that does not.

The budget looks suspiciously low

Check the match rate. A programme sized on a 95% assumed match that runs at 62% will spend its money on a fifth of the list and report an average that hides it.

It looks affordable but nothing moves

Reach is necessary and not sufficient. If coverage is good and stage movement is not, the problem is creative or offer — which is a much better problem to have, because you can now see it.

A note on the frequency input: the number that matters is impressions per person, not per account. An account with eleven people on the committee needs roughly eleven times the delivery of a one-person account to hit the same individual frequency, which is why enterprise tiers quietly starve when they share a campaign with mid-market.
